**Course Description:**
Students will explore the stages of the Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) through the lens of developers and testers to examine the characteristics, documentation, and purpose of each stage. Through applying SDLC stages within an agile development environment, students will learn the principles and best practices used to develop high quality software while also assessing the impact of communication, documentation, and ethics on the SDLC.

---

## Key Topics & Concepts

This course examines each stage of the SDLC through developer and tester lenses, applied within a Scrum-agile environment. Its work runs along two tracks. A Scrum-agile track rotates through the four Scrum team roles, one per week: Scrum Master, Product Owner, tester, and developer. A development track builds the coding skills needed for the developer week through tutorials and small assignments. The course also includes assigned group work, with team-level discussions alongside the full-class ones.

* **SDLC Models:** Waterfall's phase-gated sequence against agile's iterative delivery, and the project factors that favor each.
* **Scrum Roles, Events & Artifacts:** The Scrum Master, Product Owner, tester, and developer seats, each held for one week, plus the ceremonies and artifacts they run.
* **Requirements & Testing:** User stories with acceptance criteria, ordered into a Product Backlog, then converted into pass-or-fail test cases.
* **Estimation & Scaling:** Planning Poker, affinity grouping, and rolling-wave planning, then the Scrum@Scale, LeSS, SAFe, and Nexus frameworks.
* **Team Working Agreements:** Definition of Ready, Definition of Done, work-in-progress limits, and psychological safety as the precondition for honest inspection.
* **Java Swing Development:** ListView and Slide Show controls built in Eclipse, with generics, image scaling, and accessible caption contrast.
* **Program Essential Questions:** The CS program threads ten open-ended "essential questions" through its courses to build a computer scientist's mindset. CS-250 emphasizes three of them:
  * What does it mean to be a good team member in software development?
  * How do I interpret user needs and implement them into a program?
  * How do I approach developing programs?
* **Foundational Competencies:**
  * **CS-20409:** Explain the different methodologies and stages involved in the software development life cycle.
  * **CS-20410:** Apply the stages of the software development life cycle within an agile development environment.
  * **CS-30411:** Evaluate various software development methodologies and tools.

## Major Projects & Assignments

1. **Scrum Assignments: Scrum Events, User Stories, and Test Cases**
    * **Focus:** Three assignments across Modules Two through Four covering the core Scrum artifacts and ceremonies. Each pairs with that week's team role: Scrum Master for Scrum events, Product Owner for user stories, and tester for test cases.
    * **Scrum Events (Module Two):** Paired the Agile Team Charter with a reflection on the Daily Scrum. The charter set the SNHU Travel project's business case, mission, roles, success criteria, risks, behavior rules, and communication guidelines. Its risk register named the tight five-week window, the ambiguity of "trendy, niche," and the key-person dependency of a lean team. The reflection analyzed a filmed Daily Scrum (James, n.d.) and the facilitation behind it. The Scrum Master models candor by going first, parks tangents as sidebars, and lets the team self-facilitate. Proposed improvements: park tangents sooner, coach recurring lateness outside the meeting, and give every sidebar item an owner. Grounded the event's value in psychological safety research (Rietze & Zacher, 2025).
    * **User Stories (Module Three):** As Product Owner, turned a simulated customer focus group into a six-story prioritized Product Backlog. Each story names a user, a task, and a goal, sized small enough to estimate. Three stories carry full acceptance criteria: personalized destination recommendations, vacation preference profile settings, and a price limit filter. The priority order encodes a dependency the focus group surfaced, with preference settings landing ahead of the personalization they feed. Edge cases live in the criteria, such as users without travel history falling back to the default list.
    * **Test Cases (Module Four):** As tester, converted those three stories into pass-or-fail test cases with preconditions, steps, and expected results. Mid-assignment, the Product Owner answered a clarifying question with a wireframe that replaced the scrolling list with a slideshow. Rewrote all three cases against the new design as revised versions. The revisions verify looping Next and Previous navigation across five ranked slides. Each also records which acceptance criteria the wireframe does not yet show. Those steps test the built feature rather than the mockup.

2. **Assignment: Developing Basic ListView Control**
    * **Focus:** A Module Three coding assignment in the development track. It builds the skills needed for the developer role later in the course. The task: extend a Java Swing starter into a "Top 5 Destination List" with a picture and description per row.
    * **Approach:** Populated the list with five real destinations, each with a licensed photo credited in-code. Formatted each row through Swing's HTML label support: a bolded rank and name above a wrapping one-sentence description. Normalized every image to a fixed 160 by 120 box with a cover-scale and center-crop through `BufferedImage`. That keeps all five rows identical rectangles with no letterbox gaps. Parameterized the starter's raw `DefaultListModel` and `ListCellRenderer` with generics, deleting the runtime casts. Image loading fails fast by naming the missing file instead of surfacing a bare `NullPointerException`. Finished with row-separator borders, a neutral palette, and a scroll viewport sized to exactly five rows.

3. **Assignment: Product Application**
    * **Focus:** The Module Five development deliverable, completed while taking on the developer role for the team. The Product Owner's mid-sprint pivot to detox and wellness travel arrives here, and the Slide Show control must absorb it.
    * **Approach:** Treated the pivot as a content edit because the control isolates destination content in two lookup methods. The card layout, navigation, and panel construction never learn what a destination is. Replaced the five placeholder slides with ranked wellness destinations, from Iceland's geothermal Blue Lagoon to Baden-Baden's thermal springs. Each photo carries its Unsplash credit directly above the line that loads it, so a credit cannot drift from its image. Standardized all five captions to one format: rank and name, then a one-line description of the wellness draw. Fixed an accessibility defect in passing. Black caption text on the blue bar sat near 2.4:1 contrast, under the WCAG AA minimum. The captions became white at roughly 8.6:1. Also closed an `img` tag the original markup had left to Swing's lenient HTML parser.

4. **Role Journals**
    * **Focus:** Six journals reflect the course's rotating perspectives. They cover Scrum Master, Product Owner, tester, developer, communication practices and project management tools, and a final portfolio item.
    * **Scrum Master (Module Two):** Walked through running each Scrum event for the five-week project, favoring one-week sprints for faster feedback. Framed the role as protecting a rhythm of inspect and adapt. That means timeboxed events, visible artifacts, fast impediment removal, and follow-up on retrospective actions. Held psychological safety as the condition that makes honest reflection possible (Rietze & Zacher, 2025).
    * **Product Owner (Module Three):** Reflected on the focus group behind the user stories. Direct engagement produced knowledge solo analysis could not. One customer's objection to generic rankings reframed the product around personalization, and the group's exchange exposed the preferences-before-personalization dependency. Judged a single focus group too small and biased to stand alone. Named complementary channels: surveys, site analytics, usability testing, support tickets, and Sprint Review feedback (Kujala et al., 2022).
    * **Tester (Module Four):** Examined how acceptance criteria became pass-or-fail checks and how the wireframe exchange invalidated the first draft. Cataloged the measurable details the stories omitted. Examples: currency, tie-breaking, and whether the price filter spans the catalog or the current view. Closed with a drafted five-question clarification email to the Product Owner. Committed to flagging ambiguous criteria during backlog refinement, before writing a single case (Amna & Poels, 2022).
    * **Developer (Module Five):** Worked out what the wellness pivot requires from each teammate before code can start. The Product Owner owes an operational definition of wellness plus the list of deprioritized stories. The tester owes updated test cases first, in keeping with behavior-driven development. Described making answers cheap to give: closed questions in writing, plus a deadline anchored to a ceremony. A stated default assumption then turns silence into a decision. Argued the pivot stayed cheap because the code keeps content and rendering apart, since each changes at a different speed.
    * **Communication Practices and Project Management Tools (Module Six):** Argued transparency is a property of artifacts rather than intentions. Scrum's artifacts act as information radiators, and events only create the occasion to inspect them (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). Reread the Module Four wireframe exchange as a success of precision and a failure of reach. The answer stayed inside a two-person thread. Concluded that clarifying questions belong on the work item itself, where an issue-bound comment thread reaches the whole team (Pan et al., 2025).
    * **Final Portfolio Journal:** *To be documented as the course progresses.*

5. **Final Project: Sprint Review and Retrospective**
    * **Focus:** The capstone deliverable for the course's simulated client, "SNHU Travel." It includes a Sprint Review and Retrospective on the term's work plus an agile presentation for company stakeholders. This single project covers all three course competencies.
    * **Retrospective:** Written as Scrum Master to close the simulated company's Scrum pilot, drawing on having held all four roles. Its thesis: Scrum never removed the cost of being wrong. It moved that cost earlier, from Sprint Review into refinement and from rework into conversation. Traced one user story from a focus-group objection through ambiguity, a wireframe correction, and a mid-sprint pivot to a verified feature. Weighed the ceremony cost of one-week sprints against the inspection points they buy. The verdict endorses Scrum on one condition. The pilot succeeded because three obligations were met. The Product Owner answered inside the sprint, answers reached the whole team, and artifacts stayed worth inspecting. Recommending Scrum to the wider company means recommending those obligations, since the ceremonies are the cheap part.
    * **Presentation:** A stakeholder deck comparing waterfall and Scrum through the pilot's evidence. It shows every traditional SDLC phase surviving Agile while repeating per iteration. A wrong assumption then costs one sprint instead of one project. It replays the mid-sprint pivot under both models. It also maps six selection factors that point a project toward one approach or the other (Mishra & Alzoubi, 2023). Closes by recommending a second pilot on a fixed-scope project, so the comparison rests on two data points instead of one.

## Discussion Work & Academic Themes

The course runs seven discussions. Module Six's is a smaller discussion within the assigned group, and Module Seven has none.

1. **Introductions & Models**
    * **Focus:** Introduced professional background and compared the waterfall and agile models from a practitioner's perspective. Detailed 14 years in software engineering, a current Senior Software Engineer role, and a Certified ScrumMaster certification held since 2022. Framed the return to school as closing theory gaps left by an Associate degree in IT. Cited Donald Knuth and Leslie Lamport as the standard of rigor being pursued.
    * **Analysis:** Argued that waterfall's rigidity is its core weakness. Each phase must fully complete before the next begins, leaving little room to adjust when requirements change. Stakeholders refine their needs as a project progresses, so a waterfall product can drift far from what the customer actually needed. Also flagged the human cost. Long gaps between milestones drain the sense of progress that fuels developers. Contrasted this with agile's short sprints, which let teams adjust at each boundary, deliver value early, and bank frequent wins. Singled out the sprint retrospective as agile's most impactful practice. It creates a feedback loop of continuous improvement and a culture of shared ownership rather than blame.
    * **Follow-Up:** Clarified that "sprint" is Scrum vocabulary rather than a universal agile term. Extreme Programming calls the same concept iterations, and Kanban drops time-boxed cycles entirely in favor of continuous flow. Described the hybrid framework used at work, "Extreme Scrum," which combines all three. Scrum provides the ceremonies, Extreme Programming provides practices like pair programming, and Kanban provides continuous flow through a daily board. The team treats each day as its own sprint, opening with a stand-up where each member shares a goal for accountability.

2. **Introducing the Scrum Team**
    * **Focus:** Evaluated the Product Owner role through Overeem's (2016) white paper on the characteristics of great Scrum teams. Assessed the role from two seats held simultaneously on the same professional team: Scrum Master and senior software engineer. Each seat felt the Product Owner's impact differently.
    * **Analysis:** Of Overeem's top three Product Owner traits, argued that being empowered matters most. Scrum runs on flow, and an empowered Product Owner makes decisions quickly and keeps the roadmap clear for everyone. A disempowered one locks the roadmap in a single head, so every open question waits on an escalation. Grounded the argument in a year spent under a Product Owner who never internalized the products or customers. He said yes to every request and never told management that new work forced a tradeoff. Deadlines slipped, and the team took the blame for a promise it never made. His replacement showed the role done right. She onboarded within a month, named the team's capacity, and flagged every tradeoff early. When new work arrived, she found equal effort to defer, giving management a real choice. Her command of the system even shrank recurring Scrum Master meetings from an hour to fifteen minutes.
    * **Takeaway:** Proposed a trait the paper does not fully name: tradeoff transparency. Overeem says a great Product Owner can say no. The harder skill is keeping the cost of every yes visible to management at all times, because new work is never free.
    * **Peer Response:** Responded to a peer's post on the Development Team by extending his cross-functionality example and challenging his no-titles argument. Named the failure cross-functionality prevents: a low bus factor, the number of people who must leave before work stops. Illustrated it with an inherited service whose only deployment expert had already left, turning every release into archaeology. Then argued that deleting titles does not delete status, because an expertise gradient stays in the room after the labels go. Recounted a flattened team where design debates ended whenever the most tenured engineer spoke. Two junior engineers spotted a design flaw and never raised it. Proposed psychological safety (Edmondson, 1999) as the missing precondition that keeps disagreement cheap once titles are gone. Closed with a direct defense of titles as the durable record of an engineer's growth. Resentment comes from titles awarded poorly, so the fix is awarding them with intent rather than erasing the label.

3. **Tracking the Agile Process**
    * **Focus:** Examined the Valpak case study (Cobb, 2015) and selected the challenge of managing cross-team dependencies, the one most familiar from professional work. Valpak's systems were so integrated that most features crossed team boundaries. Rather than restructuring overnight, the company layered coordination practices onto its existing Scrum cadence. Teams tagged dependencies in Pivotal Tracker, feeding a weekly cross-team dependencies report. Proposed stories circulated a week before each new sprint. A weekly "Scrum Powwow" gathered Product Owners and Scrum Masters to review the current sprint, the next sprint, and roadmap dependencies. Teams also flagged dependencies on task boards, shared acceptance criteria early, and reserved discovery and architecture time before large cross-team epics. Impacted teams held a post-planning standup the day after sprint planning to sync stories and tasks.
    * **Analysis:** Argued the practices are Agile because they favor transparency and conversation over heavyweight coordination artifacts. The tags, board flags, and weekly report serve as information radiators, making cross-team work visible to everyone. The dependency meeting and post-planning standup put people in the same room instead of routing coordination through documents. That reflects the Agile Manifesto's preference for individuals and interactions over processes and tools. The weekly cadence also builds in inspection and adaptation, so a missed handoff costs days rather than a release. Noted that Valpak never tried to eliminate dependencies up front with a big design phase. It accepted them as a fact of an integrated system and managed them iteratively.
    * **Takeaway:** Endorsed the approach with two adjustments from professional experience. Proposed automating the dependency report, since modern trackers continuously surface linked work items between meetings. Argued that recurring dependencies are an architecture signal. When the same two teams block each other every sprint, the team or service boundary is probably drawn in the wrong place. Kept the weekly cross-team meeting but limited its agenda to dependencies that need a decision. Without that constraint, the meeting turns into a round of status updates nobody needs. Closed by affirming Valpak's core instinct: make dependencies visible, then let people talk.

4. **Amazon Case Study**
    * **Focus:** Examined Amazon's engineering culture through the "Two Pizza Rule," which caps a team at what two pizzas can feed (Leather, 2017). In practice that means about six to ten people, with each team owning a single customer segment and its products. The rule helped Amazon move fast. Small teams cut bureaucracy and paperwork, lowered costs and risks, and pushed product cycles down to about ten to fifteen days. Each team could focus on what its customers actually needed.
    * **Analysis:** Argued the rule lifts individual performance because small teams keep ownership visible and accountability clear. Responsibility never diffuses across a crowd, so effort and impact stay easy to see. That visibility encourages initiative rather than waiting for direction. Amazon pairs it with a freedom to fail, letting individuals experiment and learn without fear (Leather, 2017). The result is higher focus, more autonomy, and stronger motivation. From the alignment matrix, picked the principle of reflecting at regular intervals and tuning team behavior accordingly. Amazon applies it through Kaizen. Senior managers spend time on the front line each year to see operational problems firsthand. The customer review system feeds a second loop, routing real feedback into merchant policies and quality controls. Both practices turn everyday experience into steady improvement.
    * **Application:** Applied a second principle, welcoming changing requirements even late in development, to the SNHU Travel project. In the scenario, the Product Owner answers a tester's question with a new wireframe. It switches the top destinations page from a scrolling list to a slideshow. That is a late change, and the team welcomes it by revising the affected test cases to match. Chose the principle because it reflects real project work. Requirements shift as stakeholders learn. A team that welcomes change delivers a better product than one that locks the design too early.

5. **Estimation Process**
    * **Focus:** Chose Planning Poker as the estimation practice because it converts an estimate from a number into a conversation. Grounded the choice in years of running the practice professionally rather than in the reading alone. Described a two-pass workflow. T-shirt sizes came first as a fast ballpark across the whole backlog. Planning Poker followed on items entering the next sprint or two. The passes shared a scale. A Small landed in 1 to 3, a Medium at 5, and a Large at 8 or higher. That ordering kept the expensive conversation on work about to be built, at the cost of anchoring. A ticket already labeled Large walks into the session carrying a number someone must be willing to contradict. Summarized the mechanics from Sliger (2012). The Product Owner presents an item and does not vote. Each member privately picks from a modified Fibonacci set of 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 20, 40, and 100. Everyone reveals at once, and disagreement sends the highest and lowest voters into explanation before a revote. The cycle repeats until the numbers converge.
    * **Analysis:** Located the core benefit in the simultaneous reveal, which kills anchoring. Nobody hears the tech lead's number before forming an opinion. Called the outlier argument the real product of the exercise. When one engineer says 3 and another says 13, one almost always knows something the other does not. The gap is usually a hidden dependency, a legacy code path, or a compliance requirement. The sequence's gaps also prevent false precision, since arguing 20 versus 22 wastes breath at that uncertainty. Sliger's two-minute average per item fits a thirty-item backlog into an hour. Placed the drawbacks outside the room. Anyone absent from the discussion quietly translates points back into days, and the abstraction dies once a point equals a day. Padding returns with it. Velocity gets compared across teams, which Sliger warns against, since one team's five is not another's. The practice also needs shared team history, so a new team's early points carry little forecasting value. Genuine research work fares badly too. When nobody understands the problem yet, a timeboxed spike is more honest than a 40 card. Cited the XKCD comic on estimation: doubling a fantasy estimate three times still produces a fantasy (Munroe, n.d.).
    * **Application:** For the poorly estimated project, described leading a MySQL 5.7 to 8.0 migration on a legacy Yii 1.x PHP application. The work was scoped the way platform upgrades usually are: upgrade the engine, run the tests, fix what breaks, ship. The database work underneath turned out to be the actual project. Widespread N+1 patterns surfaced, including a paginated grid issuing 185 queries where 8 would do. High-volume reads scanned roughly four million rows to return a few hundred. OR conditions across separately indexed columns had to be split, since the old engine could not merge distinct indexes. Once-a-minute cron jobs could outrun their interval and needed locking against overlapping runs. None of it was in the estimate, because none of it was visible from the outside. Argued affinity grouping would have helped most. The project's problem was volume: hundreds of query call sites with no sense of their distribution. Affinity grouping assesses hundreds of items quickly, with members placing them on a wall relative to each other in parallel. Even a rough pass would have shown the shape of the work early. Ten large items and three hundred small ones is a very different project from fifty large ones. Named rolling-wave planning second, aimed at the commitment problem rather than the sizing problem. The first two iterations become data instead of a single estimate produced before anyone touched the new engine. Velocity from completed work then drives a forecast revised every iteration. That supports the pessimistic, likely, and optimistic ranges Sliger describes, a more honest handoff to stakeholders than a single date.

6. **Vision Quest Software Case Study** *(group discussion)*
    * **Focus:** A group activity within the assigned team, with each member acting out a Scrum role for the Vision Quest case study. Took the Product Owner role and read the case study's failures as Product Owner failures above all. Cheryl Frederick describes plans continually hijacked for short-priority developments, and Linda Ruest calls the result an illusion of progress. The team started many things while finishing very little. Argued neither failure is a coding or testing problem. They happen when nobody holds clear accountability for what the team works next and in what order. Under waterfall, priority went to whoever escalated loudest, and the development team absorbed the churn. Claimed the Product Owner seat as the single decision point: one backlog, ordered by customer value, with answerability for those choices.
    * **Analysis:** Framed Scrum as giving urgent requests a front door instead of a side entrance. When something genuinely cannot wait, the team decides together what it displaces, in the open. That visibility also erodes the single points of failure the case study worries about, since anyone can pull ordered work. Recommended two practices. First, a single ordered Product Backlog as the only intake path, covering even sales and support escalations. No request jumps the line invisibly. Second, acceptance criteria written and reviewed before Sprint Planning with at least one tester in the room. That meets Bruce Morgan's push for testers working closely with the Product Owner and building quality in early. Closed the initial post by asking developers and testers what makes a backlog item ready to pull. Also flagged that the working agreement belongs to the Scrum Master role and asked its eventual holder to template it.
    * **Follow-Up:** Posted a mid-module nudge on participation. Observed that the assignment quietly asks the team to practice what it recommends. A consensus recommendation is a team deliverable, and Agile runs on transparency and participation a discussion board cannot fake. A daily scrum with half the team absent is no daily scrum, and a silent retrospective surfaces nothing. Invited agreement, amendments, or a different direction, since any of them moves the team forward.
    * **Deliverable:** With no Scrum Master claimed, closed the week by drafting the team's consensus as a versioned working agreements document. Adapted it from templates used professionally, drawing on an active Certified ScrumMaster credential, with ownership transferring to whoever takes the role. It folded in agreements from the tester and developer teammates who contributed, three of six members. Framed the document as living, amendable at any retrospective and reviewed quarterly (Derby & Larsen, 2006). Each agreement traces back to a named case-study failure. The backlog stays the single intake, and sprint swaps happen in the open with their testing cost named. Answers live on work items rather than in private messages. Work in progress is limited (Anderson, 2010), with developers pairing on the oldest item in flight before pulling new work. Stories slice thin and vertically (Cohn, 2004), and testing runs throughout the sprint rather than batching into the final days. A Definition of Ready follows Rubin (2012) as a checklist held as a working agreement. A hard gate would rebuild waterfall approval under a new name (Beck et al., 2001). Its detail scales with risk, so payment and user-data work carries the full list. The Definition of Done belongs to the whole team and never flexes to fit a date (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). An open-items section records pending decisions: tool selection, the numeric WIP limit, continuous integration, and test-driven development (Beck, 2003). Decisions touching how developers build the Increment are recorded as support and left to the Developers.

7. **Scaling Scrum**
    * **Focus:** Addressed how scaling helps release planning by putting every team on one prioritized backlog and one shared cadence. A single team's release plan is capped by its sprint velocity. A coordinated program plans the whole product rather than one team's slice. Dependencies surface during planning rather than during release week, since teams synchronize sprints and integrate into a single increment. Customers see the payoff in volume and predictability. More teams in parallel ship larger features sooner, and a synchronized cadence lands releases on a schedule customers can trust. Warned that without a framework, adding teams mostly adds coordination cost, and integration problems eat the extra capacity.
    * **Analysis:** Chose Scrum@Scale from the four frameworks surveyed: Scrum@Scale, LeSS, SAFe, and Nexus. Jeff Sutherland designed it, and it scales by repeating Scrum rather than layering management on top (Sutherland & Scrum Inc., 2022). Teams coordinate through a Scrum of Scrums, a daily scrum held between teams. Product Owners align in a MetaScrum under a Chief Product Owner, keeping one prioritized backlog and one release plan. An Executive Action Team removes the impediments individual teams cannot. Everything practiced on the SNHU Travel project still applies at scale: the roles, the events, and the artifacts. Named SAFe the most complete alternative, fit for large regulated enterprises. Its portfolio layers and PI Planning cadence feel heavy for a company that simply needs aligned teams. PI Planning alone is a two-day event held every 8 to 12 weeks (Scaled Agile, Inc., 2026). Closed on the deciding trait: Scrum@Scale grows one team at a time and keeps the lightweight feel that makes Scrum work.

## Personal Reflection & Takeaways

I came into this course assuming it had little to teach me. I have lived the SDLC professionally for fourteen years, and I have held a Certified ScrumMaster credential since 2022. For the core mechanics, that assumption mostly held. Sprint ceremonies, backlogs, and role boundaries were review. The course still found real gaps, and they clustered where a single team's daily practice ends.

The first gap was breadth. My working knowledge centered on Scrum and Kanban, plus the Extreme Programming practices my hybrid team borrows. The course widened that survey and then went somewhere my experience never had: scaling. Scrum@Scale, LeSS, SAFe, and Nexus each answer the question of coordinating many teams on one product. I had only ever felt that problem from inside a single team blocked by another. Studying the frameworks gave me the program-level view, one backlog and one cadence across teams. Comparing them also sharpened my judgment about process weight. I can now say why SAFe fits a regulated enterprise and why Scrum@Scale fits a company that simply needs aligned teams.

The second gap was theory. I have used working agreements at two employers and written none from scratch. The Vision Quest group discussion changed that. I drafted a full agreements document for a team that had never had one, and I studied the research underneath. That meant Derby and Larsen on retrospectives, Rubin on readiness, and the Scrum Guide on the Definition of Done. The pattern repeated all term. Practices I ran on instinct turned out to have names and literature. Psychological safety research explained team dynamics I had only ever described anecdotally. Information radiators explained why answers belong on work items rather than in private threads. I could already run these practices. Now I can explain why they work and teach them with sources.
