## Discussion Work & Academic Themes

The course's two graded discussions fall in Modules One and Two. Module Eight adds an optional coding-challenges thread, which will join this list if taken as the course progresses.

1. **Introductions & Algorithms**
    * **Focus:** Introduced professional background and career direction, then wrote a short algorithm for an everyday routine. Detailed just over fourteen years in software engineering and a current Senior Software Engineer role. Stated a goal of moving deeper into systems-level and backend work, ideally writing Rust professionally. A master's in Computer Science and further mathematics study come after this degree. Named Donald Knuth and Leslie Lamport as the influence behind finishing the degree after years in industry without one. Their writing showed a level of mathematical rigor that practice alone could not reach. It also exposed a knowledge gap that made their work hard to follow.
    * **Self-Assessment:** Rated programming comfort as high, with backend work across C#, Go, Rust, PHP, and other languages. Named backend development, database design and query optimization, and performance analysis as the strongest skills. Pointed to rigorous complexity analysis as the skill most in need of improvement, the one this course targets. The intuition for which approach will run faster is already there. The mathematical vocabulary to prove it is the gap. For time management, described working ahead by finishing each module's work on the day it opens. The rest of the week then serves as a buffer for the job and anything unexpected. A consistent evening study schedule balances two courses against full-time work. Posted the introduction before opening the Module One material, a fixed habit at the start of every course. Had no questions yet as a result, and committed to reply with any before Thursday.
    * **Algorithm:** Chose the morning school drop-off and wrote it as nine ordered steps, from the wake-up call to the drive back home. One step checked that the backpack held homework and lunch before leaving. Another waited in the drop-off line until reaching the front.

2. **Data Structures: Storing a Broadcast List**
    * **Focus:** Chose a scenario from work: the list of high school football broadcasts across North America on a Friday night. The list grows all week as games get scheduled, and reads outnumber edits by a wide margin. Users browse, page, and search by school or kickoff window, and cancellations are rare.
    * **Analysis:** Argued for the vector. Its dominant operations are all fast: amortized $O(1)$ appends, $O(1)$ indexing for pagination, and $O(\log n)$ binary search over a kickoff-sorted list (Cormen et al., 2022). The linked list's advertised $O(1)$ insert and delete holds only with a pointer to the node in hand. Finding that node costs $O(n)$, and binary search is unavailable (Sedgewick & Wayne, 2011). Cited Stroustrup's run of Bentley's sorted insert-and-delete exercise, a workload the list should win. The vector still won by 50 to 100 times, because traversal and cache misses dominated (Stroustrup, 2012). A fixed array loses because the broadcast count grows every season. Noted that Knuth frames the same choice by which operations dominate (Knuth, 1997, Section 2.2). Attached a photo of the five-volume box set, also owned on Kindle.
    * **Takeaway:** Tied the choice to production, where the data lives in MySQL. MySQL's manual calls InnoDB indexes B-trees, and in practice they are B+ trees (Oracle, 2026a, 2026b). A kickoff-time lookup becomes a short tree walk and a scan of sorted leaves. That is the sorted vector and binary search applied to data too large for memory. Closed by asking peers to name an access pattern where the linked list would win.
