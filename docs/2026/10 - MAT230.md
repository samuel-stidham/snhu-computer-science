**Course Description:**
Discrete mathematics is the study of mathematical structures that are fundamentally discrete rather than continuous. That is, in contrast to the real numbers that vary continuously, the objects of study in discrete mathematics take on distinct, separated values. Topics include operations on sets, logic, truth tables, counting, relations and digraphs, functions, trees and graph theory. A significant goal of this course is to improve students' critical-thinking and problem-solving skills.

---

## Key Topics & Concepts

This course covers the discrete structures underpinning computer science across eight zyBook modules, from logic and sets through graph theory.

* **Propositional Logic:** Propositions as statements that are definitively true or false, logical operations and notation, and truth tables showing a statement and its negation. Includes translating between English statements and logical expressions and negating them with DeMorgan's laws.
* **Predicate Logic & Quantifiers:** Extending propositional logic with predicates and quantifiers, the expressions that state how many objects a statement covers.
* **Set Theory & Cartesian Products:** Translating logical operations into set operations on collections of objects, closing with Cartesian products.
* **Arguments & Proof Techniques:** Argument validity by truth table and inference rule, then direct, contrapositive, contradiction, and induction proofs.
* **Counting & Discrete Probability:** The product rule, permutations, combinations, and inclusion-exclusion, applied to event probabilities, complements, and independence. Conditional probability extends to the law of total probability and Bayes' theorem.
* **Functions & Relations:** Function equality, composition, and inverses, plus relations as arrow diagrams, matrices, and ordered pairs. Reflexive, symmetric, and transitive properties classify partial orders and equivalence relations, with Hasse diagrams and induced partitions.
* **Graphs & Trees:** Walks, trails, paths, circuits, and cycles, then Euler circuits and trails, connectivity, cut vertices, and bridges. Tree traversals, prefix codes, BFS and DFS spanning trees, and Prim's minimum spanning tree algorithm.
* **Recurrence Relations & Cryptography:** Modeling growth with recurrence relations, and RSA encryption built on modular arithmetic and the extended Euclidean algorithm.
* **Mathematical Writing in LaTeX:** Typesetting formal mathematics in TeXstudio through Codio, the workflow used for every problem set.
* **Foundational Competencies:**
  * **MAT-20659:** Apply logic and set theory in authentic problems.
  * **MAT-20660:** Solve authentic problems utilizing appropriate counting and discrete probability methods.
  * **MAT-206561:** Utilize graph theory for modeling and finding solutions.

## Major Projects & Assignments

Alongside weekly zyBooks participation and challenge activities, the graded core falls into the three groups below.

1. **Problem Sets**
    * **Focus:** Five problem sets spanning Modules One through Six, covering each module's discrete structures in depth. All were typeset in LaTeX against `mat230-macros.sty`, the rebuilt template described under External Resources.
    * **Module One (Logic & Sets):** Translated English statements into predicate logic over clinical-study and real-number domains. Negated each by driving $\neg$ inward with De Morgan's laws, then translated the results back to English. Proved quantified equivalences step by step, citing the conditional identity and double negation at each move. Evaluated nested quantifiers against a two-place predicate given as a table, arguing witness and vacuous-truth cases. Closed with Cartesian products in $n$-tuple and roster notation.
    * **Module Two (Arguments & Proofs):** Settled argument validity with truth-table counterexamples, including the converse and inverse errors. Proved a quantified argument valid by naming each inference rule: existential instantiation, simplification, existential generalization, and conjunction. Refuted its universal counterpart over a two-element domain. Wrote direct proofs on divisibility and parity, a contrapositive proof on irrational products, and a contradiction proof on averages. Proved a lowest-terms denominator claim through $\gcd$ and disproved its companion by counterexample. Finished with a full induction proof of the sum-of-squares formula, from base case through the factored inductive step.
    * **Module Three (Counting & Probability):** Counted printer assignments with the product rule, password spaces with permutations and complements, and shared-dish orders with combinations. Built a $2$-to-$1$ correspondence on recess lineups to halve $10!$, and applied inclusion-exclusion to course-enrollment data. Computed coin-flip probabilities by enumerating complements, and stack-ordering probabilities as factorial ratios in $k$. Where the assignment left a repetition restriction ambiguous, worked both readings and labeled each with its assumption.
    * **Module Five (Functions & Relations):** Decided function equality with proofs and counterexamples, composed linear functions, and confirmed $(f \circ g)^{-1} = g^{-1} \circ f^{-1}$ concretely. Converted relations among arrow diagrams, matrices, and ordered-pair sets. Classified relations by reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity, identifying a total order and an equivalence relation among weighed watermelons. Read walks of a directed graph off powers of its adjacency matrix, including a structural argument for a zero entry. Extracted minimal, maximal, and comparable elements from a Hasse diagram, then drew two diagrams from their covering relations. Described the partitions induced by equivalence relations. Drew every required diagram in TikZ rather than uploading photos.
    * **Module Six (Graphs & Trees):** Decided Euler circuits and trails by degree counts, wrote explicit circuits, and verified every edge appeared exactly once. Flagged a figure whose caption omitted a drawn edge and stated which reading each answer followed. Distinguished walks, trails, paths, circuits, and cycles with maximal examples, including a length-zero circuit. Computed connected components plus edge and vertex connectivity, separating a cut vertex from a bridge. Encoded and decoded words against a prefix-code tree. Produced pre-order and post-order traversals along with BFS and DFS spanning trees. Ran Prim's algorithm twice, once unconstrained and once forced to contain a given edge. Generalized the constrained case to an initialization change in Prim's algorithm.

2. **Exams**
    * **Focus:** Two exams anchor the course. Exam One closes Module Four, and Exam Two closes Module Seven.
    * **Exam One (Module Four):** Cumulative over the logic, proof, and counting modules. Evaluated nested quantifiers over the reals and translated statements, guarding a ratio with $y \neq 0$. Wrote a contrapositive proof through closure of the rationals and a contradiction proof on a product bound. Proved Bernoulli's inequality $(1+x)^n \geq 1 + nx$ by induction, making the unstated integer domain explicit. Finished with counting problems and lineup probabilities, including an inclusion-exclusion union in $n$ and a proof that two events fail independence.
    * **Exam Two (Module Seven):** Derived the law of total probability from the definition of conditional probability, then Bayes' theorem from both. Applied the pair to a daily-login population split across two countries. Extracted minimal, maximal, and comparable elements from a Hasse diagram. Then picked the correct divisibility diagram from four candidates by checking covering edges. Proved a model-year relation on cars is an equivalence relation and described its partition. Classified four graphs by Euler circuit and Euler trail, then ran Prim's algorithm with each step justified. Closed by modeling a fish population with a recurrence relation.

3. **Discussion Activities**
    * **Focus:** Three discussions across the term: an introduction in Module One, proofs in Module Three, and cryptography in Module Eight.
    * **Activities:**
        * **Module 1, Introduction:** Introduced professional background and framed the course around a specific goal: learning to write formal mathematical proofs. A first attempt to read Donald Knuth stalled on the language of formal proofs. Named fixing that as a personal motivation for the course and the Mathematics minor. Rated comfort with the material as reasonable, with set theory exposure from college algebra and math completed through Calculus II. Argued that everyday logic and mathematical logic share the same core. NOT, AND, and OR govern a grocery-store decision just as they govern a math problem. The difference is precision, since mathematical logic writes the same patterns in symbolic language with strict rules. Connected the study to daily engineering work, where formal logic turns experience-based reasoning about conditionals into precise reasoning. Closed by noting a stalled self-study attempt with Rosen's *Discrete Mathematics and Its Applications*. Credited course structure and deadlines as the accountability that keeps goals like this on track.
        * **Module 3, Proofs:** Compared proof by contrapositive and proof by contradiction, two techniques that share a first move and diverge after it. Both begin by negating something. A contrapositive proof replaces $p \to q$ with the logically equivalent $\neg q \to \neg p$ and proceeds directly. A four-row truth table confirms the equivalence, tying the technique back to Module One's propositional logic. A contradiction proof instead assumes $p$ and $\neg q$ together and derives some contradiction $r \land \neg r$. Contrasted their scope and endpoints. Contrapositive applies only to conditional statements but has a fixed target, so the writing stays cleaner when it fits. Contradiction applies to any statement and grants an extra hypothesis, at the cost of not knowing which contradiction will surface. Demonstrated with a worked contrapositive proof that for every integer $n$, if $n^2$ is even then $n$ is even. The direct attack stalls. The negated conclusion, $n$ odd, yields the concrete formula $n = 2k + 1$, and the result follows from definitions and algebra. Closed by inviting peers to test contradiction against the same claim. The open question was whether it works as cleanly or wraps the same algebra in an extra layer.
        * **Module 8, Cryptography:** Applied RSA with the class parameters $p = 3$, $q = 11$, $n = 33$, and $e = 3$. Posted an encrypted phrase for classmates to decode. Flagged that ciphertext values in the 27 to 31 range are encrypted letters rather than punctuation, so everything decrypts uniformly. Derived the private exponent with the extended Euclidean algorithm rather than trial and error. With $\phi(n) = 20$, back-substituting the remainders gives $7 \times 3 \equiv 1 \pmod{20}$, so $d = 7$. Verified the scheme against the assignment's "A CAB" example before posting. Closed with an observation the assignment never asks for. Twelve of the values from 1 to 32 share a factor with 33, so they have no inverse mod $n$. They still decrypt correctly. Because $n$ is a product of distinct primes, $m^{ed} \equiv m$ holds for every $m$, invertible or otherwise. The Chinese Remainder Theorem carries that argument, and it would fail if $n$ had a repeated prime factor.

## Personal Reflection & Takeaways

I came into this course with one goal, named in my first discussion post: learn the language of formal proofs. A years-old attempt to read Donald Knuth had stalled on exactly that language. This course closed that gap. I can read the symbols now. The quantifiers, the set notation, the inference rules, and the proof structures all parse. Knuth's work is finally available to me, which is what I wanted from the Mathematics minor in the first place. That makes this the single most impactful course I have taken for this degree.

The payoff reaches past reading. Proof techniques give me a way to write about my own code with rigor, arguing an invariant rather than asserting it. I expect direct proofs, contrapositives, and induction to show up in my engineering writing from here on. And some of it was simply fun. Building the RSA ciphertext by hand for the final discussion was a highlight. So was chasing Euler circuits and Euler trails through Module Six's graphs.

## External Resources

* **Required Textbook:** zyBooks. *MAT 230: Discrete Mathematics* (Southern New Hampshire University ed.). ISBN: 978-1-3941-3430-4.
* **Codio:** Cloud-based learning environment used for course work.
* **TeXstudio:** LaTeX editor introduced in the Module One tool-access activity, used for typesetting mathematical work.
* **Rebuilt LaTeX Template:** The template SNHU supplies relies on warning suppression rather than sound LaTeX. Its preamble sets `\vfuzz2pt` and `\hfuzz2pt`, which hide overfull-box reports instead of fixing the boxes. It declares the invalid theorem styles `Exercise` and `rule`, so every compile emits "Unknown theorem style" warnings. It forces vertical space with runs of `\\`, the source of its underfull-box warnings. I rebuilt it from scratch as `mat230-macros.sty`, the shared package every problem set loads. The rendered output is pixel-identical to the original. The source compiles clean with nothing suppressed, and the document flows through proper structure. Valid `amsthm` styles replace the invalid ones. Dedicated problem, parts, and step environments replace the manual `\\` spacing, with one shared length driving the vertical rhythm. A fix lands once, and every assignment inherits it. Published as [mat230-snhu-problem-set-template](https://github.com/samuel-stidham/mat230-snhu-problem-set-template).
